# Cash (UK retail savings)

**Status:** draft v1 · **Owner:** researcher agent · **Date checked:** 2026-09-21
Built per `docs/methodology.md` (v0.9, approved) and `docs/house-rules.md`. Where this template and those documents disagree, the documents win.

**Every numeric claim carries its evidence-log claim ID in brackets immediately after it**, matching a real row in `data/evidence/evidence.csv`, prefix `CASH-`.

This dossier covers UK cash as a "reference investor" asset class (methodology section 6, benchmark 2: "Cash — an easy-access savings rate series"), and three distinct retail products within it:

- **Easy-access savings accounts** (variable rate, no notice or penalty to withdraw).
- **Fixed-rate savings bonds** (1–5 year terms, rate locked for the term, usually no early access at all).
- **NS&I Premium Bonds** (not interest-bearing — a monthly prize-draw structure).

It covers ordinary retail savers using high-street/online banks, building societies and NS&I directly — not institutional deposit products, structured deposits, or offshore accounts. Cash's defining structural feature versus the other assets this company covers is **inflation drag**: a savings balance essentially never falls in nominal (money-in-the-account) terms, but reliably loses purchasing power whenever its rate sits below CPI. That mechanism, evidenced with real historical Bank of England and ONS data, is this dossier's central finding — not a single invented "cost of cash" percentage.

## 1. What it is

**Cash held in a UK bank, building society or NS&I account** is money that stays as pounds throughout — there is no unit price, no market value that can rise or fall the way a share or gold price can. What varies is the **rate of interest paid on it** (or, for Premium Bonds, the odds of winning a prize instead of interest).

- **Easy-access savings account**: a variable-rate account (from a bank, building society, or NS&I) that can be paid into and withdrawn from without notice or penalty. The rate can be changed by the provider at any time, in either direction.
- **Fixed-rate savings bond** (sometimes called a "fixed-rate bond," "term deposit" or "guaranteed growth bond"): a lump sum locked away for a stated term (typically 1–5 years) at a rate fixed for that term. In exchange for giving up access, the rate is usually higher than an easy-access account's rate at the time — for example, NS&I's own **Guaranteed Growth Bonds**, fetched directly 2026-09-21, currently offer **4.82% gross/AER** [CASH-0020] on a 1-year term (Issue 92).
- **NS&I Premium Bonds**: run by NS&I (National Savings and Investments, backed by HM Treasury), this is **not an interest-bearing account**. Instead of interest, NS&I pays a monthly **prize fund rate** — for the September 2026 draw, **4.35%, variable** [CASH-0001] — into a prize pool. Each eligible £1 Bond has odds of **21,000 to 1** [CASH-0002] of winning a prize in that month's draw, with prizes ranging from £25 up to **£1 million** [CASH-0033], and **all prizes are free from UK Income Tax and Capital Gains Tax** [CASH-0003] regardless of how much someone holds or their own tax rate. The minimum holding is **£25** [CASH-0025] and the maximum is **£50,000** [CASH-0026]. There is **no guaranteed return**: a holder might win nothing in a given month (most £1 Bonds do, in any given month, given the odds above), and the capital itself is not at risk of falling in nominal value (NS&I is 100% backed by HM Treasury), but there is no promise the prize draw will grow a given holder's money at all, let alone keep pace with inflation.

**Source and tier reasoning for NS&I claims — corrected 2026-09-21 after skeptic review**: NS&I is the seller of its own product, which under methodology section 7.1 defaults to Tier C ("a source written by someone selling the asset... is at most Tier C, unless it publishes its underlying data and method"). An earlier draft of this dossier treated NS&I as Tier B, reasoning that it is a government-owned, HM Treasury-backed body publishing a documented monthly method. A skeptic review (`reviews/cash-review-2026-09-21-v1.md`, finding 2) checked this directly: NS&I's own pages state the current prize fund rate and odds as numbers, but do not explain how either is calculated — the pages state only that they "are all variable, so they can change at any time," not the method behind them. Publishing a current output figure is not the same as publishing the underlying method, so methodology 7.1's carve-out does not actually apply. **NS&I is therefore treated as Tier C throughout this dossier**, the same as any other product seller describing its own product. Every NS&I-sourced claim below carries an `EXCEPTION APPROVED by Lucas, 2026-09-21` marker in its evidence-log row, since no Tier A or B alternative exists for NS&I's own current, product-specific terms (the same situation the marker exists for elsewhere in this repo, e.g. `GOLD-0009`).

## 2. How an ordinary UK retail investor buys it

All three products are bought **directly** from the provider — there is no broker, platform, dealer or marketplace in between, and no secondary market.

- **Easy-access and fixed-rate accounts**: opened directly with a bank, building society, or NS&I, online, by phone, by post or in a branch. The saver deposits money and it is credited to their name; there is nothing to "sell" later in the way a security is sold — the saver simply withdraws the balance (immediately for easy access; at maturity, or not at all before then, for most fixed bonds — see section 3.3).
- **Premium Bonds**: bought directly from NS&I online, by phone or by post, in £25 [CASH-0025] units up to a £50,000 [CASH-0026] per-person maximum.

## 3. The cost stack

Most rows in this stack are genuinely **"none," and here is why**: a savings account or Premium Bond holding is not bought and sold like a security. There is no dealer, no exchange, no bid-ask spread, no stamp duty, no buyer's premium, no platform standing between the saver and the provider taking a cut. This is a real structural fact about how deposit-taking works in the UK, not an assumption — it follows from section 2 above (direct provider relationship, no intermediary, no market price).

The two costs that are real and worth a full look are: **the opportunity cost of a below-inflation rate** (the defining feature of holding cash at all — this dossier's central finding, in Holding, 3.2) and **the loss of access on a fixed-rate bond before its term ends** (Selling, 3.3).

### 3.2 Holding — the real cost: inflation drag

This is the one line in the cost stack that is genuinely substantial for cash, and it is not a fee charged by anyone — it is the gap between the interest rate paid and the rate of inflation (CPI), which determines whether a saver's money can still buy as much in a year's time.

Cash's nominal balance essentially cannot fall (deposit-takers do not charge negative interest to ordinary retail savers in the UK market as observed here) — but its **real** (inflation-adjusted) value falls whenever the interest rate is below CPI. Two real, sourced snapshots show the range this actually spans:

The entire "cost of holding cash" is this gap. It is genuinely variable — sometimes small or even negative (cash ahead of inflation), sometimes very large (2022) — which is why it is shown as a range grounded in real historical data rather than a single number.

### 3.3 Selling

**Easy access**: withdrawal is the "sale" — no cost, no notice, no penalty, by definition of the product.

**Fixed-rate bonds**: this is where a real liquidity cost exists, and the evidence found in this pass points to something blunter than a simple percentage penalty — **many current UK fixed-rate bond products simply do not allow early withdrawal at all**, rather than charging a fee for it:

- **NS&I Guaranteed Growth Bonds**, fetched directly 2026-09-21, state plainly: **"No, you cannot take money out until the Bond reaches the end of its term"** [CASH-0021], and separately: **"Once you invest, you won't be able to access your money until your Bond reaches the end of its term"** [CASH-0021] — the only flexibility is a **30-day cooling-off cancellation window** [CASH-0032], not an early-withdrawal option — **Tier C** (NS&I's own product terms; see the corrected tier reasoning in section 1).
- **Skipton Building Society's** fixed-rate bonds page, fetched directly 2026-09-21, states: **"Our Fixed Rate Bonds don't usually allow withdrawals or early closure during the fixed term"** [CASH-0021] — **Tier C** (a building society's own product page, describing its own product), logged as a second, corroborating source on the same evidence-log claim as the NS&I quote above, not as sole support for a number in its own right.

A genuine attempt was made to find a current, named UK product that **does** permit early access with a quantified interest-forfeiture penalty (the "90/180 days' interest" pattern that exists elsewhere in the market) — direct fetches were attempted against Santander's and Investec's fixed-rate bond pages (both returned access errors: 404 and 403 respectively) and Close Brothers Savings (404). None succeeded in this pass.

## 4. UK tax treatment — checked 2026-09-21

### 4.1 Taxable scenario (held outside an ISA)

Interest from easy-access and fixed-rate savings accounts is taxable income. gov.uk's page "Tax on savings interest," fetched directly 2026-09-21, states the **Personal Savings Allowance**: **"Basic rate: £1,000"** [CASH-0007], **"Higher rate: £500"** [CASH-0008], **"Additional rate: £0"** [CASH-0009] — the amount of savings interest each band of taxpayer can receive tax-free each tax year — **Tier A**.

There is also a separate **starting rate for savings** for people with low other income. The same page, fetched directly 2026-09-21, states: **"If your other taxable income (not including savings interest or dividends) is less than £17,570 [CASH-0011], you can get the starter rate for savings. This means you can earn up to £5,000 [CASH-0010] of interest and not have to pay tax on it."** — **Tier A**. This threshold is internally consistent with the current Personal Allowance of **£12,570** [CASH-0031] plus the £5,000 starting-rate band, per gov.uk's "Income Tax rates" page, fetched directly 2026-09-21: **"The standard Personal Allowance is £12,570"** [CASH-0031], with the basic rate band running **"£12,571 to £50,270"** taxed at **20% [CASH-0028]**, the higher rate **"£50,271 to £125,140"** at **40% [CASH-0029]**, and additional rate income **"over £125,140"** at **45% [CASH-0030]** — **Tier A**.

**In practice**: interest above the Personal Savings Allowance (and, for low earners, above the starting rate for savings) is taxed as income at the saver's marginal Income Tax rate — 20% [CASH-0028], 40% [CASH-0029] or 45% [CASH-0030] depending on the taxpayer's band. There is no Capital Gains Tax on cash interest — CGT applies to gains on disposal of an asset, and a cash deposit's nominal value does not "gain" in that sense; interest is entirely an Income Tax question.

**Premium Bonds are different: prizes are entirely tax-free, in any scenario, with no allowance to use up.** NS&I's own page, fetched directly 2026-09-21: **"All prizes are tax-free"** [CASH-0003] and **"Any prizes you win are free from UK Income Tax and Capital Gains Tax"** [CASH-0003] — **Tier C** (see corrected tier reasoning, section 1). This is true whether or not the holder has already used their Personal Savings Allowance elsewhere, and whether the Bonds are a large or small part of the holder's savings.

### 4.2 Sheltered scenario (Cash ISA)

A **Cash ISA** shelters ordinary savings-account interest from Income Tax entirely, with no Personal Savings Allowance calculation needed. gov.uk's page on ISAs, fetched directly 2026-09-21, confirms the current annual subscription limit: **"In the 2026 to 2027 tax year, the maximum you can save in ISAs is £20,000"** [CASH-0012] — **Tier A**. This is a total across all ISA types held in the tax year, not £20,000 for cash alone.

**Premium Bonds cannot be held in an ISA** (they are not an ISA-eligible product — NS&I offers them as a separate product line) — this was not independently confirmed with a fetched NS&I or gov.uk quote in this pass, so it is treated as background, not a settled cited fact — see Open Questions. It matters less than for a taxable saver, though, since Premium Bond prizes are already entirely tax-free regardless of wrapper [CASH-0003].

### 4.3 Escalation note

No HMRC guidance found in this pass was unclear or contested on the points above (Personal Savings Allowance amounts, starting rate for savings threshold, ISA allowance, Premium Bonds' tax-free status). Nothing here is flagged to Lucas as an unresolved tax question.

## 6. Index or items, and survivorship bias

**Cash is not item-based.** There is no population of individual "items" the way there is for LEGO sets, watches or trading cards, each with its own separate price history that could be cherry-picked or suffer survivorship bias. Each product type (easy-access rate, fixed-bond rate, Premium Bonds prize fund rate) is a single published rate or mechanism at any point in time, closer in kind to an index or a benchmark rate than to a market of distinct collectible items. The item-based rules in `docs/methodology.md` section 5 — median item, share of losers, 10th/90th percentiles, a written-down sampling rule, survivorship bias in the source — do not apply here, for the same reason they do not apply to the S&P 500 as an index (`dossiers/sp500.md` section 6) or to gold's spot price (`dossiers/gold.md` section 6).

One caveat specific to Premium Bonds: because prizes are distributed unevenly (many holders win nothing in a given month; a very small number win very large prizes, up to £1 million), the *outcome for an individual holder* does behave a little like an item-based, skewed distribution, even though the *product* itself is not item-based. NS&I's own pages fetched in this pass did not provide a quotable statement on how a typical (median) holder's actual annual return compares with the advertised 4.35% [CASH-0001] prize fund rate — this is a real, mathematically plausible effect (a skewed prize distribution pulls the mean above what a small holder typically experiences), but it is not asserted here as a settled, sourced number. See Open Questions.

## 7. Risks and scams

- **FSCS limit exceeded across "sub-brands" of the same banking group.** The single most important, and most missed, real risk in cash saving is holding more than £120,000 [CASH-0004] across accounts that look like different banks but are actually the same authorised firm. FSCS's own page, fetched directly 2026-09-21, states plainly: **"If you have money in multiple accounts with multiple banks that are part of the same banking group (and share a banking licence) we have to treat them as one bank"** [CASH-0004], and **"the £120,000 compensation limit will apply to the total amount you hold across all these accounts, not to each separate account"** [CASH-0004] — **Tier A**. A saver who deliberately spreads money across what they believe are different institutions, without checking whether those brands share one banking licence, can unknowingly have less FSCS protection than they think.
- **Inflation erosion.** Cross-referencing section 3.2: cash held at a rate below CPI reliably loses purchasing power, even though the nominal balance never falls. The December 2022 example sourced above — a 0.83% [CASH-0017] sight deposit rate against 10.5% [CASH-0019] CPI — shows this can be a large effect in a single year, not just a slow, barely-noticeable drift. This is not a "scam" or a fee anyone charges; it is the defining structural risk of holding cash at all, and the reason this dossier treats it as the central cost-stack finding rather than a footnote.
- **Clone-firm and fake savings/bond scams.** The FCA's own consumer warning page, fetched directly 2026-09-21, describes the general pattern: **"Be cautious of scammers pretending to be from authorised firms. They could be clone firms (a copy of a genuine firm)"** — **Tier A**. A separate FCA page on clone firms, fetched directly 2026-09-21, states that scammers **"may offer you investments in products such as bonds, shares, foreign exchange (FX) and cryptocurrencies that are non-tradable, worthless, overpriced or even non-existent"** — **Tier A**, though this quote covers investment products generally, including bonds, rather than describing a savings-specific fraud pattern in detail. The FCA also confirms the practical consequence of dealing with an unauthorised or cloned firm: victims **"won't be covered by the Financial Ombudsman Service"** and **"won't be protected by the Financial Services Compensation Scheme (FSCS) if the firm goes out of business"** — **Tier A**. These are the FCA's own general statements about clone-firm fraud risk, not a finding against a named, specific firm; no specific alleged scam case is named in this dossier, so the house-rules requirement to use "alleged" for unproven findings does not arise here — there is nothing named to allege anything about.
- **No credit or default risk within FSCS limits.** Because deposits up to £120,000 [CASH-0004] per person per authorised firm are protected by a statutory compensation scheme, the risk of losing money outright to institutional failure is genuinely low for balances kept within that limit and spread correctly across firms that do not share a licence — a real structural difference from gold (no equivalent protection for physical bullion — see `dossiers/gold.md` section 7) and from an S&P 500 tracker (market risk, not covered by FSCS in the same way).
- **Premium Bonds: no guaranteed return, and no downside protection against inflation.** NS&I's own page states Premium Bonds are **"Not for you if you want... guaranteed returns"** [structural, per section 1] — capital is not at risk of nominal loss, but there is no promise of keeping pace with inflation, and a holder could in principle win nothing at all over an extended period, given the odds [CASH-0002].
