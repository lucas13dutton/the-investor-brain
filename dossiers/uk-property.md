# UK buy-to-let residential property

**Status:** draft v1 · **Owner:** researcher agent · **Date checked:** 2026-09-21
Built per `docs/methodology.md` (v0.9, approved) and `docs/house-rules.md`. Where this template and those documents disagree, the documents win.

**Every numeric claim carries its evidence-log claim ID in brackets immediately after it**, e.g. "5% [PROP-0006]", matching a real row in `data/evidence/evidence.csv`. No number is published without one — see `data/evidence/README.md`.

This dossier covers **one ordinary UK individual buying one additional residential property with a buy-to-let (BTL) mortgage, through an estate agent, to let privately** — the "reference investor" for this asset. It does **not** fully cost a portfolio landlord or a limited-company ("Ltd") ownership structure, both of which are real and common alternatives with materially different tax treatment (see section 8 — this is flagged for Lucas, not silently assumed away).

Several genuinely important cost lines for this asset (an average buy-to-let mortgage interest rate; a landlord's letting/management agent fee; an annual maintenance rule-of-thumb) could **not** be sourced to a quotable page this session despite a real, documented attempt against more than a dozen pages. These are published as **labelled sensitivities** under `docs/methodology.md` section 3's unsourced-cost rule, not as invented "typical" figures — see sections 3.2 and 8.

## 1. What it is

**Buy-to-let (BTL) property** is a residential house or flat bought specifically to rent out to tenants, rather than to live in. The investor's return comes from two separate sources: **rental income** (cash received each month, minus costs and tax) and **capital gain** (the difference between what the property is eventually sold for and what it cost, minus costs and tax). Unlike a fund or an index, a BTL property is a single, illiquid, physical asset — it cannot be partially sold, and buying and selling both take months, not seconds (section 3.5).

## 2. How an ordinary UK retail investor buys it

The reference investor buys one additional home through a normal UK **estate agent** (in person or via portals like Rightmove/Zoopla, which list **asking prices**, not sale prices — see section 6), usually funded by a **deposit plus a buy-to-let mortgage** from a bank or building society, arranged either directly or through a mortgage broker. The purchase completes through a **conveyancing solicitor or licensed conveyancer**, who handles the legal transfer, Land Registry registration, and paying Stamp Duty Land Tax (SDLT) on the buyer's behalf. Once bought, the property is let either **privately** (the landlord finds and manages tenants directly) or through a **letting agent**, on a let-only or fully-managed basis (section 3.2).

**A limited-company ("Ltd") structure is a real, commonly-used alternative** — many portfolio landlords buy through a company to change how mortgage interest and Capital Gains Tax are treated (a company pays Corporation Tax, and Section 24's restriction, section 4.3, does not apply inside a company in the same way). This dossier does not cost the Ltd-company route; it is flagged as an open question for a possible future pass (section 8), not silently assumed to work the same way as personal ownership.

## 4. UK tax treatment — checked 2026-09-21

Buy-to-let property is **not eligible for the Sheltered scenario in the way most other assets in this company's coverage are.** Property generally cannot be held in a Stocks & Shares ISA — gov.uk's own list of what an ISA can hold is **"shares in companies, unit trusts and investment funds, corporate bonds, government bonds, long-term asset funds"** [PROP-0046], with no mention of direct property (gov.uk, "How ISAs work", fetched directly 2026-09-21 — **Tier A**). A SIPP (self-invested personal pension) **can** hold *commercial* property, but HMRC's own Pensions Tax Manual states that an **investment-regulated** pension scheme (a defined term covering the great majority of ordinary SIPPs, not simply any "registered" scheme) acquiring **residential** property directly **"will create an unauthorised payment on the member whose arrangement acquires the asset. In addition the scheme administrator will be liable to a scheme sanction charge"** [PROP-0045] (HMRC Pensions Tax Manual, PTM125100, fetched directly 2026-09-21 — **Tier A**; qualifier corrected 2026-09-21 per skeptic review `reviews/uk-property-review-2026-09-21-v1.md`, finding 5). **This dossier therefore only covers the Taxable scenario** — it is the only realistic one for an individual buying a residential buy-to-let.

The three main tax lines interact across the property's life: **SDLT** is a one-off cost on day one (section 3.1); **Section 24** affects the ongoing tax on rental income every year the property is held; **Capital Gains Tax** applies once, on eventual disposal.

### 4.1 Income Tax on rental profit, and the Section 24 finance-cost restriction

Since the 2020/21 tax year, an individual landlord **cannot** deduct mortgage interest (or other finance costs) as an expense against rental income when calculating taxable profit. Instead, they get a **basic-rate tax reduction** — a credit, not a deduction — worth 20% of the finance cost. HMRC's own guidance, fetched directly 2026-09-21, describes the mechanism: **"The reduction is the basic rate value (currently 20%) of the lower of: finance costs...plus any finance costs brought forward; property business profits...adjusted total income"** [PROP-0042]. This was phased in gradually: in 2017/18, 75% of finance costs were still deductible as an expense and 25% [PROP-0038] was given as the new tax reduction; 50%/50% in 2018/19 [PROP-0039]; 25%/75% in 2019/20 [PROP-0040]; and from 2020/21 onward, 0% deductible, 100% [PROP-0041] as a tax reduction (gov.uk, "Changes to tax relief for residential landlords: how it's worked out, including case studies", fetched directly 2026-09-21 — **Tier A**). This rule is universally referred to in the industry as **"Section 24"**, after the section of the Finance (No. 2) Act 2015 that introduced it (confirmed via HMRC's Property Income Manual, PIM2054, fetched directly 2026-09-21: the restriction "take[s] full effect over several years between 2017/18 and 2020/21", introduced "through section 24 of the Finance (No. 2) Act 2015 and section 26 of the Finance Act 2016" — **Tier A**).

**Why this matters more than it sounds**: because finance costs are added back to taxable rental profit before the 20% credit is applied, a landlord's *taxable income* can rise sharply even though their *actual cash profit* barely moves — and that can push them into a higher Income Tax band, where the 20% credit is worth less than the relief they lost. This is not this dossier's own inference — it is HMRC's own worked example: **"John becomes a higher rate taxpayer because of the change as his total income is more than the higher rate threshold of £43,000"** [PROP-0044] — in HMRC's example, John's total income rises from £43,000 to £51,000 purely because finance costs are added back, even though his real cash profit only rises from £8,000 to £16,000, leaving him £1,600 worse off in tax (same source — **Tier A**). HMRC's own estimate is that **82% of landlords** [PROP-0043] are unaffected and have no additional tax to pay as a result of the change — meaning a minority, particularly higher-rate taxpayers and highly-leveraged landlords, are not.

### 4.2 Capital Gains Tax on sale

- **Current rates (disposals from 6 April 2026)**: **18%** [PROP-0032] on gains within the individual's basic-rate Income Tax band, **24%** [PROP-0033] above that (gov.uk, "Capital Gains Tax rates", fetched directly 2026-09-21: "you'll pay 18% on your gains" and "you'll pay 24% on your gains", both "made from 6 April 2026" — **Tier A**).
- **Whether property has its own, different rate from other assets — checked, not assumed**: as of this check, it does **not**. The same 18%/24% rates apply to residential property gains as to gains on other chargeable assets (such as the S&P 500 tracker fund covered in `dossiers/sp500.md`, which cites the identical 18%/24% rates from the same gov.uk page). Historically this was not always true: from 6 April 2024 to 29 October 2024, general-asset gains were taxed at 10%/20% while residential property gains were taxed separately, at 18%/24% [PROP-0056]; from 30 October 2024 (the Autumn Budget 2024), general-asset rates were raised to match the property rates, unifying both at 18%/24% [PROP-0056] (gov.uk, "Capital Gains Tax rates and allowances," fetched directly 2026-09-21 — **Tier A**; added per skeptic review `reviews/uk-property-review-2026-09-21-v1.md`, finding 2, which found this claim originally asserted with no citation). The two remain aligned at the current 6 April 2026 rates checked here.
- **Annual exempt amount (2026/27): £3,000** [PROP-0034] (gov.uk, "Capital Gains Tax rates", fetched directly 2026-09-21: "For the 2026 to 2027 tax year the allowance is £3,000" — **Tier A**).
- **Buy-to-let is explicitly named as a taxable disposal.** gov.uk, "Tax when you sell property", fetched directly 2026-09-21, lists **"buy-to-let properties, business premises, land, inherited property"** [PROP-0036] as examples of property CGT applies to — **Tier A**.
- **The 60-day reporting and payment deadline.** Sellers of UK property must **"report and pay any Capital Gains Tax on most sales of UK property within 60 days"** [PROP-0035] of completion (gov.uk, "Tax when you sell property", fetched directly 2026-09-21 — **Tier A**). This is a real, often-missed compliance cost/risk in its own right — see section 7. **A specific late-filing penalty amount could not be confirmed this session** (two direct fetches for HMRC's penalty schedule for this specific deadline both failed) — flagged in Open Questions §8.7.
- **Private Residence Relief (PRR) does not apply to a buy-to-let.** PRR removes CGT when you sell your own home, but only if **"you have one home and you've lived in it as your main home for all the time you've owned it"** and **"you have not let part of it out"** [PROP-0037] (gov.uk, "Tax when you sell your home", fetched directly 2026-09-21 — **Tier A**). A buy-to-let property — bought to let out, never the owner's main home — plainly fails both conditions. **This needs to be stated plainly, because it is the single most common point of confusion between "my home" and "an investment property" in UK property tax.**

### 4.3 Escalation note

**The Ltd-company ownership alternative is not costed or tax-modelled in this dossier.** A landlord who owns through a limited company pays Corporation Tax instead of Income Tax on rental profit, can generally still deduct mortgage interest as a company expense (Section 24 applies to individuals, not companies, in the same way), and faces a different CGT/dividend-extraction regime on eventual disposal or on taking money out of the company. Whether this alternative is material enough to warrant its own full dossier pass is escalated to Lucas (section 8), rather than assumed to be a minor variant of the personal-ownership route modelled above.

## 6. Index or items, and survivorship bias

Every individual house is genuinely **item-based** — no two properties are identical, unlike a share in a fund. However, unlike this company's other item-based assets (LEGO sets, watches, cards), UK residential property has a strong, official **index proxy: the UK House Price Index (UKHPI)**, which this dossier uses as its price-data source (section 5).

**Why the UKHPI does not carry the same survivorship-bias concern as a curated dealer catalogue**: the item-based sampling rules in `docs/methodology.md` section 5 exist because some sources for item-based assets only list items that still sell well, or sell easily (survivorship bias). The UKHPI is different in kind — it is built from **HM Land Registry's own record of every qualifying completed transaction** [PROP-0049], not from a curated or self-selected set of "successful" listings. A house that sells for less than it was bought for is just as much a data point in the UKHPI as one that sells for more; there is no equivalent of a dealer choosing not to list an item that lost value. The genuine limitations are different in kind from survivorship bias: it excludes certain transaction types entirely [PROP-0051] (commercial sales, below-market-value sales, Right to Buy discounts, gifts, court-ordered transfers, short leases), and it lags behind asking-price indices because it only records **completed** sales.

**This does not mean every individual property's future resale value is predictable from the index** — a specific property's condition, location and market timing still vary enormously from the national or regional average the UKHPI reports. For any future item-level analysis of specific properties (rather than the aggregate index used here), the median-item/loser-share/percentile rules in methodology section 5 would still need to be applied using HM Land Registry's Price Paid Data (section 5) as the underlying sample.

## 7. Risks and scams

- **Void periods.** Already covered as a cost (section 3.2), voids are also a real risk to plan for: a genuine period of zero rental income while the mortgage, insurance and any maintenance costs continue. The 2024 English Private Landlord Survey confirms this is a live concern for landlords, with **39% of landlords who cut rent doing so specifically to avoid a lengthy void** [PROP-0014] and **10% of single-property landlords citing void periods or reduced demand** as a market concern [PROP-0015] (MHCLG, fetched directly 2026-09-21 — **Tier A**).
- **Problem tenants, arrears and the cost/time of eviction.** If a tenant stops paying rent or a landlord otherwise needs possession, the legal process takes real time and money, during which rental income may stop. The Ministry of Justice's own statistics show the **median time from claim to repossession across all landlord possession actions in England and Wales was 27.1 weeks in April–June 2026, down from 27.9 weeks in the same quarter of 2025** [PROP-0031] (Ministry of Justice, "Mortgage and landlord possession statistics: April to June 2026", fetched directly 2026-09-21 — **Tier A**). This is an aggregate figure across all landlord types (private and social) and both standard and accelerated claims; a breakdown specific to private buy-to-let landlords was not separately available in the passage fetched — flagged in Open Questions §8.9. Legal costs (court fees, and often a solicitor) are additional to this timescale and were not independently sourced this session.
- **Leverage risk interacting with Section 24.** As set out in section 4.1, a mortgaged landlord's *taxable* income can rise sharply from the finance-cost restriction even when *cash* profit barely moves, which can — in HMRC's own worked example — push a landlord into a higher tax band and leave them meaningfully worse off despite an unchanged or even loss-making cash position [PROP-0044]. This is a structural, sourced feature of the current tax rules, not an allegation about any specific landlord or scheme.
- **Regulatory change: the Renters' Rights Act.** The Renters' Rights Act **received Royal Assent on 27 October 2025** [PROP-0052] (gov.uk, "Guide to the Renters' Rights Act", fetched directly 2026-09-21 — **Tier A**) and is intended to end Section 21 "no fault" evictions among other reforms. **The exact commencement date(s) for individual provisions (including the Section 21 abolition itself) could not be confirmed via direct fetch this session** — the guide page states only the Royal Assent date, with no commencement timeline found in the content retrieved. This is a genuinely live, moving piece of regulation for this asset class and should not be described as either "not yet in force" or "already fully in force" without a fresh check — flagged in Open Questions §8.10.
- **Missing the 60-day CGT deadline.** Because the 60-day reporting-and-payment window (section 4.2) runs from completion, not from the end of the tax year, it is easy to miss if a seller is used to the normal Self Assessment timetable. A specific penalty tariff for missing it was not independently confirmed this session (Open Questions §8.7), but the deadline itself is confirmed directly from gov.uk [PROP-0035] and is a genuine, sourced compliance risk in its own right, distinct from the underlying CGT liability.
- **General property-purchase scams and fraud** (e.g. conveyancing/solicitor impersonation fraud, where a fraudster intercepts a deposit transfer by posing as the buyer's solicitor) are a known risk in UK property transactions generally. This dossier did not independently source official (Action Fraud/police) statistics on this specific fraud type this session — flagged in Open Questions §8.11 rather than asserted without a source.
