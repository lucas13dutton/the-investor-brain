## 1. What it is

**The S&P 500** is a stock market index: a single number, calculated by **S&P Dow Jones Indices LLC (S&P DJI)**, that tracks the combined share price performance of around 500 large, US-listed companies. It is not something you can buy directly — you buy a **fund that tracks it**.

- **What it tracks**: large-cap US companies. A US Securities and Exchange Commission filing for a fund that tracks it describes the S&P 500 Index as "a widely recognized benchmark of U.S. stock market performance that is dominated by the stocks of large U.S. companies" (Vanguard 500 Index Fund/Vanguard S&P 500 ETF summary prospectus, filed with the SEC, fetched directly 2026-09-19 — **Tier B**, a regulatory-filed fund document). An earlier SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust prospectus states: "The Index is composed of five hundred (500) selected stocks, all of which are listed on national stock exchanges" (SEC filing, fetched directly 2026-09-19, filed 2013 — **Tier B**, though this specific filing is over a decade old and re-filed/updated versions were not individually checked in this pass).
- **How it is weighted**: it is widely and consistently described, across S&P DJI's own index page, fund providers' own factsheets and independent financial-education sources, as a **market-capitalisation weighted index** — bigger companies (by the value of their shares available to trade) count for more of the index's movement than smaller ones. This is corroborated across multiple independent sources cross-checked for this dossier, but S&P DJI's own methodology document (the primary source that would carry a quotable clause) returned either a "403 Forbidden" access error or unreadable binary PDF content on every direct-fetch attempt made in this pass (2026-09-19) — see Open Questions §8.1. The general "market-cap weighted" description is therefore treated as reliable background, not as a Tier A/B-quoted fact.
- **How constituents are chosen and rebalanced**: a **committee at S&P Dow Jones Indices** decides which companies are added or removed, against published eligibility rules covering company size, US-listing status, share liquidity and profitability, and the index is rebalanced on a fixed quarterly schedule. As with the weighting mechanism, this is well-corroborated across secondary sources but the exact current numeric thresholds (minimum market capitalisation, liquidity ratio, exact rebalancing dates) could not be pulled as a quoted line from S&P DJI's own methodology document in this pass — flagged in Open Questions §8.1 rather than stated as a settled number.
- **The index itself versus the funds that track it**: the S&P 500 index is just a calculation — it charges nothing and cannot be bought. What a UK investor actually buys is one of many different **funds** built to copy it as closely as possible, each run by a different manager (Vanguard, iShares/BlackRock, Fidelity, and others), each with its own cost, structure (ETF vs open-ended fund), currency share class, and small tracking difference from the index itself. These funds are not identical products even though they track the same index — this dossier treats a small number of named, real examples (section 2) as illustrative, not as the only options.

### 1.1 Price return versus total return — why this matters for this dossier

There are two different ways to measure "the S&P 500's return," and headline media figures usually mean only one of them:

- A **price return index** only tracks the share prices of the constituent companies. Dividends paid out by those companies are ignored.
- A **total return index** tracks share prices **and** assumes every dividend is reinvested straight back into the index.

Wikipedia's "Total return index" article states this plainly: "A price return index only considers price movements (capital gains or losses) of the securities that make up the index, while a total return index includes dividends, interest, rights offerings and other distributions realized over a given period of time," and separately: "The US stock index S&P 500 is an example of a price return index" (fetched directly 2026-09-19 — **Tier C**, a general encyclopedia, but this specific distinction is uncontroversial and consistent with how every index provider, including S&P DJI, defines the two series; no source disagreeing with this definition was found). This matters because **the "S&P 500" level quoted on news tickers, in most headlines, and by default on most financial data sites is the price return index — it excludes dividends.**

Per this company's methodology (`docs/methodology.md`, Decisions log, 17 Sep 2026), the global-shares benchmark used throughout this company's work is explicitly **"the S&P 500 as a total return index (dividends reinvested), converted to GBP at daily rates, with the costs of a typical low-cost UK-available S&P 500 tracker applied."**

**Implementation (Decisions log, 19 Sep 2026, resolving Open Questions §8.10):** the benchmark is calculated from the **NAV total return of Fidelity Index US Fund (P Accumulation share class, ISIN GB00BJS8SH10)** — the lowest-cost of the three funds named in section 2 (0.06% OCF), an accumulating share class (so it reinvests dividends internally, capturing total-return-equivalent performance with no investor action needed, per the Acc/Dist discussion below), and available on ordinary UK retail platforms. Its NAV total return is cited to Fidelity/Hargreaves Lansdown's own published fund data at **Tier B**, not to S&P Dow Jones Indices' own index series directly. This is a deliberate substitution: it avoids needing a commercial licence for S&P DJI's own total return index data (see section 5 and Open Questions §8.10), at the cost of the benchmark then tracking one specific fund's performance (net of that fund's own small tracking difference and costs) rather than the index's own total return series exactly. The S&P 500 index itself remains the **conceptual reference** for what this benchmark represents — a low-cost UK investor's actual total-return experience of the S&P 500, not the index's own idealised series.

**This choice was actively re-tested against iShares Core S&P 500 UCITS ETF (CSPX, IE00B5BMR087) on 2026-09-19**, the other accumulating, UK-available S&P 500 tracker named in section 2.1, against the specific test: whichever fund has the **longest continuous, downloadable, citable daily NAV history** wins, with cost and durability (fund size, provider stability) as tie-breakers. A confirmed short answer is not grounds to prefer the alternative over an unconfirmed one. This is a genuine, newly-identified gap in the benchmark's own evidence base (see Open Questions §8.10) rather than a clean win for either fund, and is flagged for Lucas: see the escalation note at the end of section 5.

This substitution is a deliberate, different choice from the headline price-only figure, for the reasons below:

- **Which one a fund actually delivers to a holder depends on the share class, not the index.** An **income (distributing) unit** pays dividends out to the investor as cash — economically similar to holding the price return index plus a cash dividend stream. An **accumulation unit** (or an "Acc"/accumulating ETF share class) reinvests dividends automatically inside the fund — economically similar to holding the total return index. Either way, a long-term holder who reinvests (or whose fund reinvests for them) captures dividends; a holder who spends the cash distributions from an income unit, or who only ever looks at a price-only chart, does not.
- **Total return is the more honest comparison for "what a UK investor's money actually did."** A genuine, sourced illustration of the scale of the gap: a widely repeated S&P Dow Jones Indices-attributed figure states that $1 invested in the S&P 500 on 1 January 1930 would have grown to **66.48 by the end of 2012 on a price-return basis, versus 1,832.45 on a total-return basis with dividends reinvested** — however, this specific figure could not be traced to a directly fetchable, quotable page from S&P DJI itself in this pass (the underlying research PDF returned unreadable binary content), so it is reported here as a widely-cited illustration of the scale of the effect, not as an independently verified Tier A/B number — see Open Questions §8.2. The qualitative point (total return is dramatically higher than price return over long periods, because of decades of compounding reinvested dividends) is not in serious dispute across any source checked.
- **Practical consequence for this dossier**: any NRR ("Net Real Return") figure this company ever publishes for the S&P 500 benchmark, or for a UK S&P 500 tracker fund held by a retail investor, must be built on a **total return** series, not the price-only headline figure — otherwise it understates what a real investor holding an accumulating fund (or reinvesting an income fund's distributions) actually experienced. This dossier does not itself publish a return figure (no window or cost scenario is calculated here), consistent with house rules — it only establishes which series is right to use.

## 2. How an ordinary UK retail investor buys it

### 2.1 Route A — an ETF tracking the S&P 500

Through a standard UK investment platform or stockbroker (e.g. AJ Bell, Hargreaves Lansdown, interactive investor, InvestEngine, Freetrade), inside a General Investment Account (GIA), a Stocks & Shares ISA, or a SIPP — bought and sold like a share, at a live price, during UK trading hours. Real, currently-available examples on the London Stock Exchange:

- **Vanguard S&P 500 UCITS ETF (USD) Distributing** (ticker **VUSA**, ISIN IE00B3XXRP09, GBP-quoted line on the LSE). Vanguard's own product page for this share class, fetched directly 2026-09-19, states its Ongoing Charges Figure as **"0.07%"**, describes the underlying index as one where "the Index is comprised of large-sized company stocks in the US," and gives its distribution frequency as **"Quarterly"** with a distribution history showing a most recent payment of **"US$0.3096 per unit"** — **Tier B** (fund manager's own published page). **Correction from round-1 review: VUSA is a Distributing share class, not Accumulating.** It pays cash out to the investor roughly every quarter rather than reinvesting it inside the fund. Per section 1.1, a VUSA holder only gets total-return-equivalent performance (the series this company's methodology benchmarks against) if they manually reinvest those cash distributions themselves — an investor who spends the distributions, or simply lets them sit as cash, is economically closer to holding the price-return series plus a separate cash income stream, not the total-return series. Vanguard's accumulating equivalent is a different, distinct fund: **S&P 500 UCITS ETF (USD) Accumulating** (ticker **VUAG**, ISIN IE00BFMXXD54). Vanguard's own product page for VUAG, fetched directly 2026-09-19, states: **"All dividends are reinvested for the 'Accumulation' shares"** — **Tier B**. VUAG carries the same 0.07% OCF as VUSA. This dossier keeps VUSA as the flagship Route A example because it is the older, larger and more commonly referenced of the two tickers in UK retail commentary — but a reader specifically wanting the automatic, no-action-needed total-return-equivalent performance this company's methodology assumes should look at VUAG or CSPX (accumulating — see below), not VUSA, unless they intend to reinvest VUSA's cash distributions themselves.
- **iShares Core S&P 500 UCITS ETF** (ticker **CSPX**, **accumulating**). iShares' own product page, fetched directly 2026-09-19, states the fund's objective: **"The Fund seeks to track the performance of an index composed of 500 large cap U.S. companies"** — **Tier B**. Its ongoing charge could not be read directly from the iShares factsheet PDF (which returned as unreadable binary content), so it is instead confirmed via justETF's fund-profile page, fetched directly 2026-09-19, which states: **"0.07% p.a."** — this is the fund's own published figure, reproduced by an aggregator, in the same way this company's gold dossier sourced iShares' gold ETC's OCF; the tier assigned (**B**) reflects that it is a published, audited fund figure, accessed via an intermediary because the primary PDF would not render as text. Unlike VUSA, CSPX reinvests dividends internally, so a CSPX holder captures total-return-equivalent performance without needing to do anything themselves.
- **Fidelity Index US Fund** (P Accumulation share class, ISIN GB00BJS8SH10) — an open-ended fund rather than an ETF (see Route B below), included here because it is one of the most commonly held UK S&P 500 trackers. Hargreaves Lansdown's own fund page, fetched directly 2026-09-19, states its ongoing charge as **"0.06%"**.

These are illustrative, not exhaustive — other providers (Invesco, HSBC, SPDR/State Street, L&G) also offer UK-available S&P 500 trackers; their specific costs were not individually pulled for this pass (see Open Questions §8.3).

### 2.2 Route B — an open-ended index fund (OEIC/unit trust) tracking the S&P 500

Through the same kind of platform, or directly through the fund manager's own retail platform (e.g. Vanguard Investor, Fidelity Personal Investing), inside a GIA, ISA or SIPP. Unlike an ETF, there is no live market price — you buy and sell at a single daily valuation (see section 3.5). Example: **Fidelity Index US Fund** (above). Vanguard also offers its US 500 exposure as an OEIC-style fund on some platforms, distinct from its VUSA ETF; this was not separately verified in this pass.

**Note on source tiers used throughout section 2**: general structural descriptions (how buying works, ISA/SIPP eligibility, GBP vs USD share classes) are cross-checked across multiple issuer and platform pages. Each individually is Tier B (issuer) or Tier C (platform), but they are consistent with each other, so are treated as reliable background — never as the sole support for a cost or return figure, per house rules B5.

### 3.4 Currency

The S&P 500's constituent companies are priced in **US dollars**, so currency conversion is embedded in this investment somewhere — the question is where, and whether it is charged separately to the UK investor.

- **GBP-denominated share classes/listings (e.g. VUSA's and CSPX's GBP lines on the LSE)**: the ETF trades and settles in GBP throughout. There is **no separate, itemised FX charge to the investor** for buying or selling the GBP-quoted line — market makers and authorised participants handle the underlying USD/GBP conversion as part of making a market in the security, and their cost of doing so is presumed to be reflected in the bid-ask spread rather than billed as a separate line item. No source was found that separately quantifies this embedded cost (the same limitation this company's gold dossier found for gold ETCs) — flagged in Open Questions §8.1. The underlying USD/GBP exchange-rate risk is a **market-price risk** (the value of US shares in GBP terms moves with the exchange rate), not a transaction cost, and is not itemised here for that reason. **This bullet covers the buying/selling mechanics only, which are the same for VUSA and CSPX. It does not cover VUSA's own quarterly cash distributions, which raise a separate FX question — see the next bullet.**
- **USD-denominated listings or share classes**: where an investor instead buys a USD-quoted line, or an accumulating/OEIC fund's USD share class rather than its GBP-hedged or GBP-quoted equivalent, an explicit platform FX charge applies. AJ Bell's own international-investing page (as previously fetched for this company's gold dossier, same tiered structure applies here) states its FX charge tiers starting at 0.75% and falling to 0.25% above £20,000; Hargreaves Lansdown's own overseas-share-dealing page, fetched directly 2026-09-19, states: **"£0 - £10,000: 0.99%"**, **"£10,000 - £25,000: 0.50%"**, **"Over £25,000: 0.20%"** — **Tier C** (platform-published). HL's page also states, on foreign-currency income: amounts received in foreign currency are converted "to sterling at the interbank rate plus an extra... 1% spread" — this was fetched from HL's general overseas-shares help content, and its specific applicability to a USD-denominated fund distribution (rather than a directly-held US share dividend) was not separately confirmed — flagged in Open Questions §8.4.
- **Accumulating share classes** (such as **CSPX and VUAG** — the type most UK investors buying an S&P 500 tracker purely for growth, with no manual reinvestment effort, would hold) never generate a cash dividend for the investor to convert at all — the fund reinvests USD dividends internally, so no investor-level FX transaction happens on distributions for these share classes. **This does not describe VUSA.** VUSA is a Distributing share class (section 2.1) and does pay a quarterly cash distribution. Whether that distribution is paid to a GBP-quoted-line VUSA holder already converted into GBP, or in USD requiring a separate platform conversion (and at what rate), was not confirmed for VUSA specifically in this pass — flagged in the expanded Open Questions §8.4.

## 4. UK tax treatment — checked 2026-09-19

Both routes (ETF and open-ended fund) are treated identically for UK tax purposes, because both are simply funds holding shares — the tax rules do not distinguish between an ETF wrapper and an OEIC wrapper.

### 4.1 Taxable scenario (held outside an ISA/SIPP)

- **Capital Gains Tax (CGT) on disposal.** Selling units/shares in an S&P 500 tracker fund for more than you paid is a disposal for CGT purposes, in the same way as selling any other share or fund. gov.uk's "Capital Gains Tax" guidance, fetched directly 2026-09-19, confirms CGT applies to **"[any shares](/tax-sell-shares) that are not in an ISA or PEP"** and that **"You do not pay Capital Gains Tax on certain assets, including any gains you make from: ISAs or PEPs"** (confirming the contrast with the Sheltered scenario below) — **Tier A**.
- **Current CGT rates and allowance (tax year 2026/27).** gov.uk's "Capital Gains Tax rates" page, fetched directly 2026-09-19, states: **"you'll pay 18% on your gains"** within the basic-rate Income Tax band, and **"you'll pay 24% on your gains"** above that, both "made from 6 April 2026"; and **"For the 2026 to 2027 tax year the allowance is £3,000"** — **Tier A**.
- **Income Tax on dividends (income/distributing units, held outside a wrapper).** Cash distributions from an income share class are taxed as dividend income. gov.uk's "Tax on dividends" page, fetched directly 2026-09-19, states: **"You also get a dividend allowance of £500 each year"** and gives the current rates: **"Basic rate: 10.75%, Higher rate: 35.75%, Additional rate: 39.35%"**, for **"the rates from 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027"** — **Tier A**. A companion gov.uk publication on the rate change, fetched directly 2026-09-19, confirms the increase: **"The ordinary rate will rise from 8.75% to 10.75%, and the upper rate from 33.75% to 35.75%"** from April 2026, with the additional rate unchanged at 39.35% — **Tier A**.
- **Accumulation units do not avoid this tax.** Where a fund reinvests dividends automatically rather than paying them out (an accumulating share class), HMRC still treats that reinvested amount as taxable income in the year it arises, even though the investor receives no cash. HMRC's Capital Gains Manual, CG57707, fetched directly 2026-09-19, states: **"The notional distribution is treated as allowable expenditure where it is subject to Income Tax in the hands of the unit holder"** — **Tier A**. This confirms the notional distribution is subject to Income Tax (matching general UK tax guidance for OEIC/unit trust accumulation units) and that the same amount is then added to the investor's cost basis for CGT purposes, preventing double taxation on eventual disposal.

### 4.2 Sheltered scenario (ISA and SIPP)

- **Stocks & Shares ISA.** gov.uk's "How ISAs work" page, fetched directly 2026-09-19, states that a Stocks and Shares ISA can hold **"shares in companies, unit trusts and investment funds, corporate bonds, government bonds, long-term asset funds"** — both an ETF (a fund whose units trade like shares) and an open-ended fund (a unit trust/OEIC) fall within this description. The same page confirms the annual allowance: **"Every tax year you can save up to £20,000 in one account or split the allowance across multiple accounts"** — **Tier A**. Gains and income inside the ISA are outside CGT and dividend tax (per the CGT exemption quote in 4.1).
- **SIPP.** gov.uk's own guidance on pension tax confirms contributions attract tax relief: **"Your private pension contributions are tax-free up to certain limits"** — **Tier A**, fetched directly 2026-09-19. On growth inside the pension specifically, HMRC's Capital Gains Manual, CG67650, fetched directly 2026-09-19, states directly: **"The disposal of an investment held for the purposes of a registered pension scheme is not a chargeable gain"** and **"It is therefore exempt from capital gains tax"** — **Tier A**. This confirms CGT-free growth inside a SIPP. Withdrawals from a SIPP are taxed as income under separate rules not explored in depth here, as this dossier is scoped to the accumulation/holding phase.
- **Contrast with physical gold**: unlike physical gold bullion (which cannot be held in an ISA or SIPP at all, per this company's gold dossier), an S&P 500 tracker fund — whether an ETF or an open-ended fund — is a mainstream, fully ISA- and SIPP-eligible investment. This is one of the starkest cost/tax differences between the two asset types this company covers.

### 4.3 Escalation note

No HMRC guidance found in this pass was unclear or contested on the core points above (CGT on disposal, dividend tax on income units, notional-distribution tax on accumulation units, full ISA/SIPP eligibility). One open point: the exact mechanics of how a fund reports a "notional distribution" to an individual investor (timing, and whether it is shown on a consolidated tax certificate or must be separately obtained from the fund's own reporting) were not independently checked against a specific platform's own investor-facing documentation — this is a practical administration question, not a substantive tax-rate question, so it is listed in Open Questions (§8.6) rather than escalated to Lucas as a genuine HMRC ambiguity.

## 6. Index or items, and survivorship bias

The S&P 500 is an **index-type asset**, not an item-based one, per `docs/methodology.md` section 5: it is one calculated series (or, more precisely, two related series — price return and total return, as explained in section 1.1), not a population of individual items each with its own separate history. There is no median-item calculation, no survivorship-bias sample to build, and no "share of items that lost money" figure of the kind used for LEGO sets or watches — **the item-based rules in methodology section 5 do not apply here.**

A different, index-specific version of survivorship bias is worth noting for completeness, though it is a property of the index's own construction rather than of this dossier's data sourcing: the S&P 500's committee-based selection process **removes companies that shrink, go bankrupt or are acquired, and replaces them with larger, more successful ones** (see the eligibility criteria discussed in section 1, to the extent they could be sourced). This means the index's own long-run historical return already reflects a form of survivorship at the constituent level — a company that failed is dropped, not carried forward at a near-zero value the way an individual failed stock would be. This is a well-known, structural feature of any market-cap-weighted, committee-maintained large-cap index (not unique to the S&P 500), and is one of the points documented critics raise (see section 7 below) when discussing what the index's headline long-run return does and does not represent. This point is flagged as background/context rather than as a quantified bias correction, because quantifying its exact effect on long-run returns would require a specific academic study, which was not sourced in this pass — see Open Questions §8.7.

For the two named tracker funds and the OEIC example, the relevant "index" for each is the fund's own NAV, which tracks the S&P 500 (price or total return, depending on structure) minus the fund's own charges and tracking difference — again a single series per fund, not a sample.

## 7. Documented criticisms and risks

### 7.1 US concentration — the S&P 500 is not "global shares"

Although this company's methodology uses the S&P 500 as its stand-in benchmark for "global shares" (`docs/methodology.md` section 6), the S&P 500 itself is **100% companies domiciled and primarily listed in the United States**. A UK investor using an S&P 500 tracker as their only equity holding is taking concentrated exposure to one country's stock market, its currency, its regulatory environment and its economic cycle — not a globally diversified portfolio, despite the S&P 500's size and prominence. No single source was needed to establish this basic structural fact (it follows directly from the index's own eligibility rules requiring US companies), but it is worth stating plainly because the methodology's choice to use the S&P 500 as a "global shares" proxy is a simplifying convention, not a claim that the S&P 500 is itself internationally diversified.

### 7.2 Top-heavy weighting — a small number of names drive an increasing share of the index

Because the index is market-capitalisation weighted, the largest companies count for a disproportionate and, in recent years, growing share of its total value and movement.

**Headline figure — resolved, Decisions log 19 Sep 2026, closing Open Questions §8.13.** Per methodology §7.1's new rule that research published by a firm selling investment or advisory services is Tier C by default, RBC Wealth Management's and Goldman Sachs Asset Management's own figures (below) can no longer carry this section's headline number, whether alone or combined. Instead, the headline is attributed directly to the primary source both firms cite: **S&P Dow Jones Indices' own "S&P 500 Factsheet," as of 31 March 2026, which Goldman Sachs Asset Management's published insights article quotes in its own footnote as stating the top 10 stocks account for "36.5% of the index by market capitalization"** (Goldman Sachs Asset Management insights article, fetched directly 2026-09-19, footnote citing "S&P 500 Factsheet," S&P Dow Jones Indices, as of March 31, 2026) — **Tier B**. This is tiered B, not A, because it is S&P DJI's own official factsheet figure accessed indirectly (via GSAM's accurate footnote quote, since S&P DJI's own site could not be fetched directly in this pass — see Open Questions §8.1), the same treatment this dossier gives iShares' CSPX OCF when sourced via justETF rather than iShares' own unreadable factsheet PDF (section 2.1).

This means index-level performance, in recent years, has been driven to an unusually large extent by a small number of very large companies (commonly reported as concentrated in mega-cap technology), rather than by the broad average of 500 companies the index's name implies.

**RBC Wealth Management's figure — directional corroboration only, not the headline.** RBC's own published analysis (using FactSet data), fetched directly 2026-09-19, states: **"The 10 largest companies by market cap (including IBM, Exxon, General Electric and Philip Morris) made up roughly 19 percent of the index"** in 1990, and **"By the end of 2015, the top 10 stocks accounted for about 19 percent of the S&P 500's weight and roughly 19 percent of total index earnings"** — but **"By the end of 2025, the 10 largest companies accounted for nearly 41 percent of the S&P 500's total weight,"** sourced by RBC to **"RBC Wealth Management, FactSet; data as of 12/31/25."** Under the new rule, RBC — a firm selling investment advisory services generally — is **Tier C by default**. It names a primary source (FactSet) and an as-of date, which per the rule lets it support a **directional** claim (concentration has risen sharply since 1990) but never stand as the headline number itself. It is shown here as corroborating context for the direction and rough scale of the change, not as the cited figure for "how concentrated is the index today."

Several other press figures for current concentration (e.g. "40.7% in 2025," "top 10 at 37.8% as of August 2026") were found but could not be traced to a fetchable, quotable primary line in this pass and are not cited (see Open Questions §8.8).

**Tier classification of RBC and GSAM — resolved by policy, not case-by-case.** Both are wealth/asset-management businesses that sell investment advisory services generally. Per methodology §7.1's new rule (19 Sep 2026), this makes both Tier C by default regardless of whether they disclose a data source and as-of date — that disclosure only earns them the narrower "directional, non-headline" carve-out, not full Tier B status. The round-1/round-2 approach of treating GSAM as a "second independent source" alongside RBC is superseded: the new rule explicitly states two such sources do not make each other independent. This dossier now uses GSAM's citation only as the pointer to the true primary source (S&P DJI's own factsheet), not as a Tier B authority in its own right.

### 7.3 How people lose money in this asset

- **Buying at a peak and needing to sell in a downturn.** The S&P 500 has fallen by 50%+ peak-to-trough on more than one occasion historically (e.g. 2000–2002, 2007–2009); a UK retail investor who bought near a peak and was forced to sell during a subsequent fall — rather than holding through it — can realise a large loss. This dossier does not itself calculate a specific "largest fall" or "worst window" figure (that belongs in a future NRR calculation using a licensed total-return series, per section 5), but the general risk is well established and not contested by any source.
- **Currency risk.** Because the underlying companies are priced in USD, a GBP investor's return depends partly on the GBP/USD exchange rate, not only on US share prices. A period of dollar weakness against sterling can reduce — or a period of dollar strength can flatter — the GBP return a UK holder actually experiences, independent of how the underlying US companies perform. This is a market-price risk (see section 3.4), not a transaction cost, but it is a genuine way to lose money relative to expectations formed by watching only the USD-denominated headline index level.
- **Clone-firm and unauthorised-platform scams.** The FCA's own "Warning List" guidance and press material describe a specific, well-documented fraud pattern relevant to any mainstream investment product, including S&P 500 trackers: fraudsters impersonate a real, authorised firm. The FCA's press release on this, **dated 27 January 2021 and still live on fca.org.uk as of the date it was fetched directly for this dossier, 2026-09-19**, states: **"Clone firms are fake firms set up by scammers using the name, address and 'Firm Reference Number' (FRN) of real companies authorised by the FCA"** and advises: **"Always check the FCA Register to make sure you're dealing with an authorised firm and check the FCA Warning List of firms to avoid"** — **Tier A** (regulator's own published warning). **This is a five-year-old release, not a new or current alert** — it is cited here because the underlying "clone firm" pattern it describes is evergreen and the FCA continues to host it unamended, but the risk should be read as a general, recurring pattern that this scam type follows, not as evidence of a live, current wave of S&P 500-specific clone-firm activity. Fraudsters have specifically cloned firms offering index-tracking and general investment products, not just complex derivatives — checked generally, not against a named S&P 500-specific clone case in this pass.
- **Platform/broker failure — what is and is not protected.** If a regulated UK platform holding an investor's ETF or fund units fails, the Financial Services Compensation Scheme (FSCS) provides some protection, but this is **not** protection against the investment itself falling in value. The FSCS's own page, fetched directly 2026-09-19, states the protection limit: **"If the firm failed after 1 Apr 2019 - up to £85,000 per eligible person, per firm"** and separately makes clear what is excluded: **"We can't accept any claims that are for poor investment performance - unfortunately, the nature of investments means their value can go down as well as up."** — **Tier A**. This distinction — FSCS protects against firm failure and bad advice, never against ordinary market losses — is an important one for a UK retail investor to understand about any mainstream fund, including an S&P 500 tracker.
- **Tracking difference and tracking error.** A tracker fund is not guaranteed to exactly match the index it follows — its own costs (the OCF, plus trading costs inside the fund), sampling method, and any securities-lending income it earns or foregoes can all cause it to slightly under- or over-perform the index itself over time. This is a real, if usually small, way a fund's return can differ from "the S&P 500's return" as reported in the media — not covered by a specific quoted figure in this pass (see Open Questions §8.9).
- **Compliance note**: this dossier names several specific funds, tickers and companies (Vanguard/VUSA, Vanguard/VUAG, iShares/CSPX, Fidelity Index US Fund, AJ Bell, Hargreaves Lansdown, InvestEngine, S&P Dow Jones Indices, FCA, FSCS, RBC Wealth Management, Goldman Sachs Asset Management). Per house-rules Part 4, any mention of a specific listed company, fund or coin goes to Lucas before publication. This is flagged here so it is not missed downstream.
