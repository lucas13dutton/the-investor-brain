## 1. What it is

**Gold** is a precious metal that has been used as money, jewellery and a store of value for millennia. As an investment asset today it is bought either as a **physical object** (a coin or a bar you or a custodian holds) or as a **paper claim on physical gold** (a fund-like security traded on a stock exchange). Its price is set globally, in US dollars per troy ounce, on wholesale bullion markets — UK retail prices are that global price plus a markup.

- **Coins**: UK-minted bullion coins such as the **Britannia** (1 oz, .9999 fine gold, face value £100, UK legal tender) and the **Sovereign** (approx. 0.2354 oz, .9166 fine gold, face value £1, UK legal tender). Also widely sold: Krugerrands (South Africa), American Eagles, Canadian Maple Leafs — none of these are UK legal tender.
- **Bars**: cast or minted bars from refiners such as the Royal Mint, PAMP, Perth Mint, Metalor, typically 1g to 1kg for retail buyers, usually .999 or .9999 fine.
- **ETC (Exchange Traded Commodity)**: a security, listed on a stock exchange, backed by physical gold bars held in a vault by a custodian. Legally it is a debt security, not a fund, but for a retail buyer it behaves like a share — one price, bought and sold through a broker, no need to arrange storage or delivery.

## 2. How an ordinary UK retail investor buys it

### 2.1 Physical gold

Through online bullion dealers (e.g. BullionByPost, Chards, Atkinsons Bullion, physicalgold.com), the Royal Mint's own shop, or — less commonly — a coin fair or a specialist auction house (e.g. Spink, Baldwin's of St James's, Noonans Mayfair). Payment is normally by bank transfer or card; delivery is by insured courier, or the buyer can pay to leave the metal in the dealer's vault ("allocated storage"). Buying at auction is a real but distinctly more expensive route (see section 3.1) and is not the ordinary channel — most retail buyers buy direct from a dealer or the Mint.

### 2.2 Gold ETFs / ETCs

Through a standard UK investment platform or stockbroker (e.g. Hargreaves Lansdown, AJ Bell, interactive investor, Freetrade), inside a General Investment Account, a Stocks & Shares ISA, or a SIPP, in the same way as buying a share or ETF. No physical delivery, no separate storage arrangement — the platform holds it in your account, usually in a nominee structure. Examples on the London Stock Exchange, quoted in GBP: **iShares Physical Gold ETC (SGLN)**, **Invesco Physical Gold ETC (SGLD)**, **WisdomTree Physical Gold (PHGP)**, **Royal Mint Physical Gold ETC (RMAU)**.

**Note on source tiers used throughout this section**: general product descriptions above (specs, structure, how buying works) are cross-checked across issuer pages (iShares, Invesco, Royal Mint) and dealer sites (BullionByPost, Chards). Each individually is Tier C (a seller or issuer describing its own product), but they are consistent across independent sources, so are treated as reliable background, never as the sole support for a cost or return figure.

### 3.4 Physical gold — Currency

**None, with a stated reason and a caveat.** UK bullion dealers quote and settle entirely in GBP: the buyer never sees or pays a separate foreign-exchange charge. Any cost the dealer incurs converting its USD-priced wholesale gold into GBP is the dealer's own cost, not a line item charged to the retail buyer. We could not find any dealer disclosure that separately quantifies this embedded FX cost — it is presumably folded into the dealer premium and buy-back spread figures already captured in sections 3.1 and 3.3, rather than being additional to them. Because we cannot verify that assumption with a source, it is flagged, not asserted as settled — see Open Questions §8.1.

## 4. UK tax treatment — checked 2026-09-18

### 4.1 Physical gold: Capital Gains Tax

- **UK legal-tender gold coins are exempt from CGT.** HMRC's Capital Gains Manual states that "coins and bank notes which are sterling currency are not chargeable assets" (**CG76881**, confirmed by direct fetch). A separate manual page specifically confirms which gold coins qualify: **CG78305** states verbatim, "Sovereigns minted in 1837 and later years and Britannia gold coins are currency but, like all sterling currency, are exempt because of TCGA92/S21(1)(b)." **This page was fetched directly for this rebuild and confirms the exact wording** — it replaces the earlier draft's reliance on dealer-marketing pages for the "1837" cut-off, which is now sourced to HMRC directly. **Source: HMRC internal manuals CG76881 and CG78305 — Tier A.**
- **This does not apply to**: gold bars, gold wafers, non-UK legal-tender coins (Krugerrand, American Eagle, Canadian Maple Leaf, pre-decimal coins no longer legal tender), and jewellery. These are ordinary chattels; if sold for more than £6,000, CGT applies to the gain (subject to the general chattels marginal-relief rules and the annual exempt amount). **Source: HMRC chattels guidance, general CGT rules — Tier A.**

### 4.2 VAT on physical gold

- Investment-grade gold coins and gold bars/wafers of at least 995 fineness are **VAT-exempt**. A coin qualifies if it is minted after 1800, at least 900 fineness, is or has been legal tender in its country of origin, and is normally sold at no more than **180% of the open market value of its gold content** (the "180% test," confirmed verbatim against gov.uk guidance for this rebuild). **Source: HMRC Notice 701/21A / gov.uk "Investment gold coins and VAT Notice 701/21A," checked 2026-09-18 — Tier A.**
- Gold below 995 fineness, jewellery, and numismatic coins priced well above the 180% threshold are **not exempt** and are subject to standard-rate VAT (20%) if bought new from a VAT-registered UK seller.

### 4.3 Gold ETFs/ETCs: Capital Gains Tax and Stamp Duty

- Gains on gold ETCs held outside an ISA/SIPP are subject to CGT in the normal way — there is **no** currency-style exemption for ETCs, because the security itself, not physical coins, is what is disposed of.
- **Annual CGT exempt amount (2026/27): £3,000** per individual, not carried forward.
- **CGT rates (disposals from 6 April 2026)**: 18% on gains within the individual's basic-rate income tax band; 24% above that. **Source: gov.uk "Capital Gains Tax rates," checked 2026-09-18 — Tier A.**
- Gold ETCs listed on a recognised stock exchange **can be held inside a Stocks & Shares ISA or SIPP**, sheltering gains under the "Sheltered" scenario. Physical gold coins and bars **cannot** be held in an ISA. This was **not independently confirmed ISIN-by-ISIN** against each platform's own eligible-instrument list — see Open Questions §8.5.
- **Stamp Duty Reserve Tax (SDRT) does not apply — corrected mechanism, and a corrected citation.** The earlier draft attributed this to ETCs being "structured as debt securities," which is likely the wrong reason. A skeptic review (2026-09-18) then found that the page cited to fix that — STSM011020 — was itself wrong: that page is an introductory "What is Stamp Duty Reserve Tax?" page and contains no incorporation test at all. The correct page is **HMRC's Stamp Taxes on Shares Manual, STSM031090** ("Scope of Stamp Duty Reserve Tax (SDRT): chargeable securities — general"), fetched directly for this fix. It states that securities fall **outside** the definition of "chargeable securities" where they "are issued or raised by a company incorporated outside the UK (and with no UK register)" and are not paired with shares in a UK company — while bringing a non-UK company's securities back into scope where they are "issued or raised by a non-UK incorporated company but registered in a register kept in the UK." Read together, this confirms UK incorporation (or, failing that, a UK share register) as the general test for whether a company's securities are "chargeable securities" for SDRT. The gold ETC that could be checked in this pass — iShares Physical Gold ETC (SGLN), issued by iShares Physical Metals plc — is **incorporated in Ireland** (ISIN IE00B4ND3602, confirmed against the issuer's own prospectus/factsheet), with no UK share register found on the facts checked. Non-UK incorporation, not the debt-vs-equity distinction, is the more likely reason SDRT does not apply. **This has only been verified for the iShares issuer**; Invesco, WisdomTree and Royal Mint's ETC issuers were not individually checked for incorporation in this pass — see Open Questions §8.6. **Sources: HMRC Stamp Taxes on Shares Manual, STSM031090 (Tier A) for the general chargeable-securities rule, fetched directly 2026-09-18; iShares Physical Metals plc prospectus/factsheet (Tier B) for the specific incorporation fact.**

### 4.4 Escalation note

The CGT exemption for UK legal-tender coins (Britannia, post-1837 Sovereign) is now well established with a direct HMRC citation (CG78305), closing an escalation the earlier draft raised unnecessarily. One point remains genuinely open rather than resolved: whether the exemption could ever be challenged by HMRC on valuation grounds (e.g. a coin sold for a large premium over face value, arguably straying from "currency" in substance). No evidence of this being tested was found, but it also was not ruled out. Per house-rules escalation criteria ("any tax question where HMRC guidance is unclear" goes to Lucas), this is noted for Lucas rather than resolved here.

## 6. Index or items, and survivorship bias

Gold (as bullion/spot metal) is an **index-type asset**, not an item-based one: one underlying commodity price applies (the LBMA Gold Price), not a population of individual items each with its own history. There is no median-item, no survivorship-bias sample to build, and no "share of items that lost money" calculation of the kind used for LEGO sets or watches — the item-based rules in methodology section 5 do not apply to gold itself.

The exception is **numismatic/collectible gold coins** (rare dates, proof finishes, graded coins), which behave more like an item-based collectible market and would need their own item-sampling approach if ever covered. This dossier treats only bullion-grade coins and bars (priced close to metal content), not numismatic coins, and flags numismatic coins as **out of scope** here.

For gold ETCs, the relevant "index" is the fund's own NAV, which tracks the LBMA Gold Price minus the fund's charges — again a single series, not a sample.

## 7. Risks and scams

- **Unauthorised/unregulated dealers and outright investment scams.** Fraudsters cold-call victims — often elderly — offering gold, rare-earth-metal or "precious metal investment" opportunities that either do not exist or are not what was promised; some schemes claim to hold bullion in a secure vault that does not exist. A separate, related pattern is police-impersonation "courier fraud," where a caller poses as a police officer or bank official and persuades the victim to buy gold or jewellery to hand to a "trusted courier" as part of a fake investigation. **Source note (correcting the earlier draft): the earlier draft cited "actionfraud.org.uk" as a Tier A source. That domain is a private fraud-recovery/claims-advice company, not the official police service, and should not be cited as an official source.** The genuine official UK reporting service is run by the **City of London Police** at `actionfraud.police.uk`, recently superseded for public reporting by `reportfraud.police.uk` (transition completed December 2025). Direct automated fetches of `actionfraud.police.uk` returned an error (403) in this pass, for both the researcher and the earlier skeptic review — **this page's exact wording needs a human to check it in a browser before it is quoted directly; flagged in Open Questions §8.9.** In the meantime, the general scam pattern above is corroborated by City of London Police's own press releases (fetched successfully), e.g. its reporting on Hatton Garden courier-fraud crackdowns and a boiler-room fraud case where 84 gold bars were seized from a convicted fraudster. **Source: City of London Police press releases — Tier A** (official police source); Action Fraud page content — unverified pending a human check.
- **Physical gold investment itself is not FCA-regulated.** Buying bullion coins or bars from a dealer is not a regulated activity, so there is no Financial Services Compensation Scheme (FSCS) protection and no Financial Ombudsman Service route if a dealer fails or defrauds you. The Advertising Standards Authority has repeatedly ruled that ads for physical gold investment are **misleading if they fail to disclose this unregulated status** — for example, its ruling against Montford Group Ltd (trading as "Britannia Bullion") over a March 2026 LinkedIn ad, upheld June 2026. **Source: ASA ruling, June 2026 — Tier A** (regulator finding, independently confirmed live on asa.org.uk).
- **Counterfeit coins and bars.** Sovereigns and Britannias are among the most-counterfeited gold coins in the world because of their recognisability; convincing fakes exist using tungsten cores (which can pass simple weight checks) as well as gold-plated base metal. Buying only from the Royal Mint, LBMA-accredited refiners/dealers, or reputable dealers who offer authentication, and checking weight/dimensions/a ring test, reduces but does not eliminate this risk. **Source: dealer authentication guides (Chards, Bleyer Bullion) — Tier C**, consistent across multiple independent dealers but not an official statistic.
- **High-pressure sales tactics and inflated "collectible" pricing.** Some sellers (including door-to-door, phone, or "free gold guide" lead-generation ads) push commemorative or "limited edition" coins at prices far above their gold content, exploiting the CGT-free/VAT-free status as a hook. The ASA rulings above are part of a wider regulatory pattern of concern about this kind of promotion.
- **Storage and custody risk (physical).** Home-stored gold is exposed to theft/loss and is often under-insured, since standard contents insurance caps "valuables" cover well below typical bullion holdings (see section 3.2). Third-party vault storage removes this risk but introduces counterparty risk (the storage provider's solvency and controls) and an ongoing cost.
- **Counterparty and physical-backing risk (ETCs).** A physically-backed ETC is only as good as (a) the custodian's actual holding of allocated bars matching the fund's stated assets, and (b) the fund structure holding up in the issuer's insolvency. Major providers (iShares, Invesco) publish bar lists and undergo independent audits, which is standard mitigation, but this dossier has **not independently verified** a specific audit report — flagged in Open Questions.
- **Price transparency.** Dealer buy and sell prices for physical gold are set by each dealer, not centrally, so an inexperienced buyer/seller can unknowingly transact well away from the LBMA benchmark price, especially for less-liquid items (small fractional coins, older/numismatic pieces). ETCs are more transparent because their market price and NAV are visible in real time on the exchange.
- **Compliance note**: this dossier names numerous specific funds and companies (iShares SGLN, Invesco SGLD, WisdomTree PHGP, Royal Mint RMAU, BullionByPost, Chards, AJ Bell, Hargreaves Lansdown, eBay, BullionVault, Spink, Baldwin's, Noonans, and — in a negative/regulatory context — Montford Group Ltd t/a Britannia Bullion). Per house-rules Part 4, any mention of a specific listed company, fund or coin, and any scam/fraud content, goes to Lucas before publication. This is flagged here so it is not missed downstream.
